\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Continual Learning and Experience Replay}
\label{subsec:experience_replay}

Methods against catastrophic forgetting fall into a few families: replay, parameter and functional regularisation, optimisation-based methods, context-dependent processing, and template-based classification \cite{van_de_Ven_2025}. This thesis focuses on replay, specifically its simplest formulation: Experience Replay (ER) \cite{chaudhry2019tinyepisodicmemories}. At each step, ER draws a replay mini-batch from a buffer of stored past examples, combines it with the current-task mini-batch, and takes one optimiser step on the summed loss.

A standard approach to managing this episodic memory is reservoir sampling \cite{vitter1985random}, which ensures that every sample seen so far occupies a buffer slot with equal probability. Under this setup, the buffer remains empty during the first task, meaning ER reduces to plain SGD; consequently, performance gaps and forgetting metrics are evaluated from the second task onward. Despite this simplicity, ER remains highly competitive: a small episodic memory often outperforms purpose-built methods and scales to longer sequences \cite{chaudhry2019tinyepisodicmemories}. Furthermore, because the ER update consists of exactly two ingredients, it provides a remarkably clean substrate for isolating and studying interventions at task boundaries.

However, a finite buffer is inherently an imperfect estimator of the past-task distribution. \citet{aljundi2019gradientbasedsampleselection} frame buffer selection as approximating the feasibility region of the full past data, with reservoir sampling serving as one specific policy within that framework. Consequently, the replay gradient derived from a small buffer will differ in both direction and magnitude from the true gradient of all past data.

\subsection{The Stability Gap}
\label{subsec:stability_gap}

\citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} identified the stability gap using continual evaluation, measuring past-task accuracy after every update rather than only at task boundaries. In this view a new task begins with substantial but transient forgetting that the model subsequently recovers from, and they quantify it with worst-case metrics, mainly the minimum past-task accuracy reached during training, rather than with end-of-task averages alone. The effect is not confined to one method: they observe it across experience replay, constraint-based replay, knowledge distillation, and parameter regularisation. It also persists well beyond the standard benchmarks, appearing in joint incremental learning of homogeneous tasks \cite{kamath2024expandingscopestabilitygap} and in continual pre-training of large language models \cite{guo2024efficientcontinualpretrainingmitigating}, and several mitigations have been proposed \cite{soutifcormerais2023improvingonlinecontinuallearning, harun2024overcomingstabilitygapcontinual}.

Two findings in this literature anchor the account of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}. First, \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} trace the initial drop to a gradient imbalance at the boundary: the new-task gradient dominates the first updates, so the optimiser trades stability for plasticity precisely when the past task is most exposed. Second, the gap is a property of the trajectory rather than the objective. \citet{hess2024complementaryperspectivescontinuallearning} show it persists under training on the exact joint loss with full access to all past data, and \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess} demonstrate the geometric mechanism analytically: steepest descent from an old-task minimum leaves the old basin even with perfect joint gradients, because the steepest direction and the direction to the joint minimum disagree. A perfect objective therefore fails to prevent the gap, and the descent geometry itself must be addressed.

\subsection{One Update, Many Methods}
\label{subsec:path_finding}

Beyond plain ER, several methods constrain the update at the task boundary using information about past tasks. GEM stores past-task gradients and projects the new-task gradient onto the nearest direction that leaves every stored loss unchanged or lower \cite{lopezpaz2022gradientepisodicmemorycontinual}. A-GEM replaces the per-task constraints with a single averaged past-task gradient, reducing the projection to one cheap operation \cite{chaudhry2019efficientlifelonglearningagem}. EWC adds a quadratic penalty around the past optimum weighted by a curvature estimate \cite{Kirkpatrick_2017}, and natural-gradient approaches precondition the update with past-task curvature so that steps avoid directions the old task depends on \cite{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}.

On the surface these methods look unrelated, but \citet{sodhani2022introductionlifelongsupervisedlearning} show the replay-based ones share a single update. Every step combines two gradients, the current task's and the replay buffer's, under two scalar weights,
\begin{equation}
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\big(\alpha_1 \nabla L_{\text{current}} + \alpha_2 \nabla L_{\text{replay}}\big),
    \label{eq:unified_update}
\end{equation}
so a method reduces to a rule for choosing $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. Plain ER fixes both to one. A-GEM holds $\alpha_1 = 1$ and raises the replay weight $\alpha_2$ only when the new-task and stored gradients conflict, by just enough to cancel the interference, so its projection is equivalent to re-weighting the two losses; GEM does the same per stored task, through a quadratic program whose multi-constraint solution no longer reduces to two scalar weights. MEGA \citep{guo2020improvedschemesepisodicmemorybased} likewise holds $\alpha_1 = 1$ but sets the replay weight from a loss ratio, $\alpha_2 = L_{\text{replay}}/L_{\text{current}}$, leaning on memory once the new task is already fit.

The same reading applies to the $\lambda$-curriculum of this thesis. It fixes $\alpha_2 \equiv 1$ and turns the other knob, ramping the current-task weight $\alpha_1 = \lambda(t)$ up from zero. It belongs to the same family as A-GEM and MEGA; what distinguishes it is which weight it moves and what signal drives the weight. The adaptive variant (Section~\ref{subsec:gates}) is the mirror image of MEGA: MEGA raises the \emph{replay} weight from a \emph{loss} ratio to recruit memory once the new task is learned, while the adaptive curriculum holds the \emph{new task} back with a \emph{gradient-norm} ratio, letting it in only as the replay gradient reasserts itself. Section~\ref{subsec:gates} generalises Equation~\eqref{eq:unified_update} from scalar weights to operators and organises all of these methods, together with asymmetric PER, along two design axes.

\subsection{Low-Loss Paths and Mode Connectivity}
\label{subsec:lmc}

\emph{Mode connectivity} gives empirical backing to the \emph{monotone reference path} introduced in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}: the route from the old-task solution $\theta_A^*$ to the joint solution $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$ along which the past-task loss only ever rises toward its final value, rather than first climbing above it and coming back down as the bowed descent does. For such a path to be usable, the two solutions must be joined by a corridor of low loss in the first place, and this is what the mode-connectivity literature reports. \citet{garipov2018losssurfacesmodeconnectivity} and \citet{draxler2019essentiallybarriersneuralnetwork} showed that independently trained minima of a deep network are joined by curved paths of near-constant low loss, and \citet{frankle2020linearmodeconnectivitylottery} sharpened this to straight paths for networks that share early training. For continual learning specifically, \citet{mirzadeh2020linearmodeconnectivitymultitask} found that the sequentially trained and the multitask solutions often lie in the same basin, connected by a straight low-loss line. A benign, low-loss route from $\theta_A^*$ to $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$ should therefore exist in trained networks. Where it does, the stability gap reflects the optimiser straying off that route, and an intervention succeeds by keeping it there.
