\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Continual Learning and Experience Replay}
\label{subsec:experience_replay}

Methods against catastrophic forgetting fall into a few families: replay, parameter and functional regularisation, optimisation-based methods, context-dependent processing, and template-based classification \cite{van_de_Ven_2025}. This thesis focuses on replay, specifically its simplest formulation: Experience Replay (ER) \cite{chaudhry2019tinyepisodicmemories}. At each step, ER draws a replay mini-batch from a buffer of stored past examples, combines it with the current-task mini-batch, and takes one optimiser step on the summed loss.

A standard approach to managing this episodic memory is reservoir sampling \cite{vitter1985random}, which ensures that every sample seen so far occupies a buffer slot with equal probability. Under this setup, the buffer remains empty during the first task, meaning ER reduces to plain SGD; consequently, performance gaps and forgetting metrics are evaluated from the second task onward. Despite this simplicity, ER remains highly competitive: a small episodic memory often outperforms purpose-built methods and scales to longer sequences \cite{chaudhry2019tinyepisodicmemories}. Furthermore, because the ER update consists of exactly two ingredients, it provides a remarkably clean substrate for isolating and studying interventions at task boundaries.

However, a finite buffer is inherently an imperfect estimator of the past-task distribution. \citet{aljundi2019gradientbasedsampleselection} frame buffer selection as approximating the feasibility region of the full past data, with reservoir sampling serving as one specific policy within that framework. Consequently, the replay gradient derived from a small buffer will differ in both direction and magnitude from the true gradient of all past data.

\subsection{The Stability Gap}
\label{subsec:stability_gap}

\citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} identified the stability gap using continual evaluation, measuring past-task accuracy after every update rather than only at task boundaries. In this view a new task begins with substantial but transient forgetting that the model subsequently recovers from, and they quantify it with worst-case metrics, mainly the minimum past-task accuracy reached during training, rather than with end-of-task averages alone. The effect is not confined to one method: they observe it across experience replay, constraint-based replay, knowledge distillation, and parameter regularisation. It also persists well beyond the standard benchmarks, appearing in joint incremental learning of homogeneous tasks \cite{kamath2024expandingscopestabilitygap} and in continual pre-training of large language models \cite{guo2024efficientcontinualpretrainingmitigating}, and several mitigations have been proposed \cite{soutifcormerais2023improvingonlinecontinuallearning, harun2024overcomingstabilitygapcontinual}.

Two findings in this literature anchor the account of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}. First, \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} trace the initial drop to a gradient imbalance at the boundary: the new-task gradient dominates the first updates, so the optimiser trades stability for plasticity precisely when the past task is most exposed. Second, the gap is a property of the trajectory rather than the objective. \citet{hess2024complementaryperspectivescontinuallearning} show it persists under training on the exact joint loss with full access to all past data, and \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess} demonstrate the geometric mechanism analytically: steepest descent from an old-task minimum leaves the old basin even with perfect joint gradients, because the steepest direction and the direction to the joint minimum disagree. A perfect objective alone therefore fails to prevent the gap, and the descent geometry itself must be addressed. The optimiser matters as well: comparing optimisers in a similar setting, \citet{obis2025optimizersstabilitygap} find the depth and duration of the drop to depend substantially on which one is used, an early indication that the effective step size is a lever on the gap.

\subsection{One Update, Many Methods}
\label{subsec:path_finding}

Beyond plain ER, several methods constrain the update at the task boundary using information about past tasks. GEM stores past examples and, at every step, recomputes each past task's gradient on its stored data, then projects the new-task gradient onto the nearest direction that leaves every stored loss unchanged or lower \cite{lopezpaz2022gradientepisodicmemorycontinual}. A-GEM replaces the per-task constraints with a single averaged past-task gradient, reducing the projection to one cheap operation \cite{chaudhry2019efficientlifelonglearningagem}. EWC adds a quadratic penalty around the past optimum weighted by a curvature estimate \cite{Kirkpatrick_2017}, and natural-gradient approaches precondition the update with past-task curvature so that steps avoid directions the old task depends on \cite{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}.

These methods are not all of one kind: EWC and the natural-gradient family act through a penalty or a preconditioner rather than through replayed data, and they do not fit the form below. The replay-based methods, however, share a single update, as \citet{sodhani2022introductionlifelongsupervisedlearning} show. Every step combines two gradients, the current task's and the replay buffer's, under two scalar weights,
\begin{equation}
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\big(\alpha_1 \nabla L_{\text{current}} + \alpha_2 \nabla L_{\text{replay}}\big),
    \label{eq:unified_update}
\end{equation}
so a replay method reduces to a rule for choosing $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$. Plain ER fixes both to one. A-GEM holds $\alpha_1 = 1$ and raises the replay weight $\alpha_2$ only when the new-task and stored gradients conflict, by just enough to cancel the interference, so its projection is equivalent to re-weighting the two losses; GEM does the same per stored task, through a quadratic program whose multi-constraint solution no longer reduces to two scalar weights. MEGA \citep{guo2020improvedschemesepisodicmemorybased} likewise holds $\alpha_1 = 1$ but sets the replay weight from a loss ratio, $\alpha_2 = L_{\text{replay}}/L_{\text{current}}$, leaning on memory once the new task is already fit.

The same reading applies to the $\lambda$-curriculum of this thesis. It fixes $\alpha_2 \equiv 1$ and turns the other knob, ramping the current-task weight $\alpha_1 = \lambda(t)$ up from zero. It belongs to the same family as A-GEM and MEGA; what distinguishes it is which weight it moves and what signal drives the weight. The adaptive variant (Section~\ref{subsec:gates}) is the mirror image of MEGA: MEGA raises the \emph{replay} weight from a \emph{loss} ratio to recruit memory once the new task is learned, while the adaptive curriculum holds the \emph{new task} back with a \emph{gradient-norm} ratio, letting it in only as the replay gradient reasserts itself.

\subsection{Low-Loss Paths and Mode Connectivity}
\label{subsec:lmc}

\emph{Mode connectivity} gives empirical backing to the \emph{monotone reference path} introduced in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}: a route from $\theta_A^*$ to $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$ along which the past-task loss only ever rises toward its final value requires the two solutions to be joined by a corridor of low loss, and this is what the literature reports. Curved paths of near-constant low loss join independently trained minima \citep{garipov2018losssurfacesmodeconnectivity, draxler2019essentiallybarriersneuralnetwork}, straight ones join networks that share early training \citep{frankle2020linearmodeconnectivitylottery}, and, for continual learning specifically, the sequentially trained and multitask solutions often lie in the same basin \citep{mirzadeh2020linearmodeconnectivitymultitask}. Where such a route exists, the stability gap reflects the optimiser wandering off it, and an intervention succeeds by keeping it there.
