\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

This section describes the empirical testbed (Section~\ref{subsec:testbed}), the metrics that summarise each run (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}), and the full set of conditions (Sections~\ref{subsec:overview}--\ref{subsec:cifar}) that test the predictions of Sections~\ref{sec:motivation} and~\ref{sec:theory}. Each experimental block maps to one of the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:proposed_approach} and to a falsifiable prediction derived earlier.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

\subsubsection{Testbed}
\label{subsec:testbed}

The experiments run on two testbeds of different scale. A small, fast testbed: rotated MNIST on a two-layer MLP. It carries the dense mechanistic sweep, where the gap is probed step by step across many conditions and seeds. A larger testbed: a domain-shift CIFAR-10 on a CIFAR-adapted ResNet-18. This tests whether the findings survive a deeper backbone and a stronger task shift, the regime where the smoothness assumption behind the bound of Section~\ref{sec:theory} weakens. Both testbeds share one design choice: the label space and task structure are held fixed and only the input distribution shifts, and the headline sequences use the minimal two tasks, so there is exactly one transition and exactly one instance of the stability gap, with no interference between overlapping gaps.

\paragraph{Datasets.}

The small benchmark is rotated MNIST (rot-MNIST). Following \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning}, rotations are measured from a $-90^\circ$ baseline: the first task $T_0$ presents the digits at $-90^\circ$, and the second task $T_1$ applies a further $90^\circ$ rotation to bring them to $0^\circ$. Each task uses the standard $60{,}000$ training and $10{,}000$ test images. The $90^\circ$ rotation makes the two input distributions well separated while keeping the tasks structurally identical, with the same label space and the same underlying digit identities. A five-task variant (rotations $[-90,-60,-30,0,30]^\circ$) is used for the long-sequence block of Section~\ref{subsec:longseq}.

The large benchmark is a domain-shift CIFAR-10: $T_0$ uses clean CIFAR-10 and $T_1$ uses the Gaussian-noise corruption at severity $3$. The ten classes are shared, so as in rot-MNIST only the input distribution shifts and two tasks produce a single transition. A three-task variant ($[\text{none}, \text{Gaussian noise}, \text{shot noise}]$) and two alternative corruptions are used in the generalisation block of Section~\ref{subsec:cifar}.

\paragraph{Models.}

Each benchmark has its own backbone. The rot-MNIST sweep uses a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with ReLU activations (Table~\ref{tab:mlp}); the CIFAR-10 block uses a CIFAR-adapted ResNet-18 (Table~\ref{tab:resnet}).

\subsubsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{subsubsec:eval_protocol}

Throughout training, every task's test accuracy is evaluated every $10$ steps. At each task transition the cadence increases to every step, so the dip is recorded at full resolution where it occurs. The two benchmarks differ only in how long this per-step cadence is held. On rot-MNIST it runs for the entire new task: one online epoch is short (${\approx}234$ steps), so the whole post-transition trajectory is captured per step. On CIFAR-10, where a task spans ten epochs, the per-step cadence is held for the first $50$ steps after the transition, enough to cover the gap, and then drops back to every $10$ steps for the rest of the task.

A stability-gap tracker snapshots the pre-task accuracy on each past task before the transition and records the resulting (step, accuracy) series, from which it computes the scalar metrics of Section~\ref{subsubsec:gap_metrics}. A gradient tracker records, at the same per-step cadence, the directional and magnitude fidelity of the replay-buffer gradient against the deterministic empirical past-task gradient $g_{\text{true}}$ (Section~\ref{subsubsec:grad_metrics}); it is enabled for the decomposition conditions only.

\subsubsection{Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

All metrics are recorded automatically and aggregated later. Unless noted otherwise, each is reported as the seed mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over a condition's seeds (five for the rot-MNIST and CIFAR headline blocks, three for the CIFAR generalisation block); \texttt{gap\_area\_end} is reported as a seed mean only, and \texttt{recovery\_steps} only when a recovery occurs within the record. This section explains the most important ones; the complete definitions of every logged metric are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:metric_defs}.

\paragraph{Stability-gap metrics.}
\label{subsubsec:gap_metrics}

These are computed from the dense per-step series to summarize the performance gap along two complementary axes: how severe the drop is and how long it lasts. Let $a_j^{\text{pre}}$ denote the baseline accuracy on past task $j$ before the switch, and $a_j(t)$ denote its accuracy at step $t$ of the new task. The first metric, \texttt{gap\_depth}, captures the single worst point of instability across the observation window:
\begin{equation*}
    \texttt{gap\_depth} = \max_j \left( a_j^{\text{pre}} - \min_t a_j(t) \right).
\end{equation*}
The second metric, \texttt{gap\_area\_end}, quantifies the total transient cost of the dip by measuring how far and for how long the accuracy falls below its eventual recovered level. To calculate this, we use a reference point $b_j = \min\!\left(a_j^{\text{pre}},\, \bar{a}_j\right)$, where $\bar{a}_j$ is the stable accuracy the task eventually settles at. The \texttt{gap\_area\_end} then integrates the shortfall $\max\!\left(0,\, b_j - a_j(t)\right)$ over the duration of the new task and sums it across all past tasks. By anchoring to the recovered level rather than the pre-task baseline, we isolate temporary dips from permanent forgetting. If a task permanently degrades to a lower plateau without a sharp dip, it settles at $\bar{a}_j < a_j^{\text{pre}}$ and contributes approximately zero to the gap area. Only a true temporary dip registers here, while permanent loss is measured separately using standard ACC/FORG metrics. Therefore, \texttt{gap\_depth}, \texttt{gap\_area\_end}, and ACC/FORG cleanly decouple how deep a drop is, its cumulative cost before recovery, and the permanent damage it leaves behind. Finally, \texttt{recovery\_steps} measures the gap temporally: once a past task dips, this metric counts the steps required for every past task to climb back to within $90\%$ of its pre-task baseline (remaining undefined if no drop occurs). Precise definitions of every logged metric, including the secondary and legacy measures not used in the main analysis, are collected in Table~\ref{tab:gap_metrics}.

\paragraph{Continual-learning metrics.}

To place the gap in the standard continual-learning frame, we also report the continual-evaluation metrics of \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning}, computed from the task-boundary accuracy matrix $R[i,j] = A(E_j, f_{T_i})$ that is logged for every run (so any downstream metric can be reconstructed). The two that carry the argument are average final accuracy (ACC), which checks that a curriculum buys lower depth without an accuracy cost, and the worst-case measures min-ACC and WC-ACC, which report retention at its lowest point rather than only at the end. FORG and the windowed worst-case forgetting/plasticity measures $\mathrm{WF}_w$/$\mathrm{WP}_w$ are reported for completeness; all definitions are in Table~\ref{tab:cl_metrics}.

\paragraph{Gradient-fidelity diagnostics.}
\label{subsubsec:grad_metrics}

For the decomposition block the gradient tracker compares the replay-buffer gradient $g_{\text{replay}}$ against the deterministic empirical past-task gradient $g_{\text{true}}$ (a separate full-data forward/backward pass, no sub-sampling). Two diagnostics measure how faithfully a finite buffer approximates the past data \citep{aljundi2019gradientbasedsampleselection}: the directional fidelity $\cos(g_{\text{replay}}, g_{\text{true}})$ and the magnitude fidelity $\|g_{\text{replay}}\|/\|g_{\text{true}}\|$. These supply the direct per-step evidence for the estimator-bias contributor of Section~\ref{subsec:decomp}. A third, $\|g_{\text{new}}\|/\|g_{\text{replay}}\|$, is the update-side magnitude asymmetry; its inverse $\|g_{\text{replay}}\|/\|g_{\text{new}}\|$ is the signal the adaptive curriculum of Section~\ref{subsec:curriculum} uses to set $\lambda(t)$. Logged names and exact definitions are in Table~\ref{tab:grad_metrics}.

Each diagnostic step adds one forward and backward pass over every past-task training sample ($60{,}000$ for rot-MNIST $T_0$), which is affordable on the MLP testbed but would dominate runtime on larger backbones, so the toggle stays off for the curriculum and CIFAR blocks, whose claims rest on \texttt{gap\_depth}, \texttt{gap\_area\_end} and ACC rather than per-step gradient geometry.

\paragraph{Statistical comparison.}

Across conditions we compare metrics with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the seed-paired difference. Seeds are paired by index across conditions, so the per-seed deltas $\Delta_s = m(C_a, s) - m(C_b, s)$ factor out the seed-induced variance that would otherwise dominate the five-seed sample. We prefer Wilcoxon over the paired $t$-test because \texttt{gap\_depth} and accuracy are bounded in $[0,1]$ and typically right-skewed near zero, so the $t$-test's normality assumption is suspect; the Wilcoxon test exchanges that assumption for the milder one that $\Delta_s$ is distributionally symmetric under the null. For equivalence-style claims, such as ``the adaptive curriculum matches the best linear curriculum'', we report the seed-paired difference with its bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval rather than a null-hypothesis test.

\subsubsection{Three-Contributor Decomposition}
\label{subsec:decomp}

\paragraph{Aim.} Isolate the three contributors of Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition}: magnitude asymmetry ($G_{\text{mag}}$), estimator variance ($G_{\text{est}}$), and the trajectory effect ($G_{\text{traj}}$). To then quantify each one's share of the total gap. This is the diagnostic question of RQ1.

\paragraph{Design.} Two of the three contributors are predicted to be downstream symptoms of the discontinuity at the task boundary; the third, estimator variance, is a property of finite replay buffers and persists at any schedule. Removing each contributor by construction and observing the residual gap identifies which contributors drive the dip. The path G1 $\rightarrow$ G2 $\rightarrow$ G3 $\rightarrow$ G4 removes one source at a time (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}).

        NCL (reference)             & none             & K-FAC precision preconditioning & ---                              & path-finding reference \citep{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess} \\

\paragraph{Gradient balancing.} The balanced conditions G3 and G4 compute the replay and new-task gradients in two separate backward passes, normalise each to unit norm, and combine them $50/50$, so the two terms enter the update with equal magnitude regardless of their raw sizes. This targets the first-step asymmetry of Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition}: at $\theta_0^*$ the replay gradient is near zero, so an unbalanced step is driven almost entirely by $g_{\text{new}}$, whereas after normalisation the replay direction carries equal weight from the first step. Balancing removes the magnitude asymmetry by construction, so $G_{\text{mag}}$ is exactly the gap-depth difference between an unbalanced step (G1) and a balanced one (G3).

\paragraph{Full-data conditions.} The full-data conditions G2 and G4 hold the entire past-task training set in the buffer and, at every step, compute the replay gradient on every stored sample exactly once. The resulting replay gradient is the deterministic empirical past-task gradient at the current parameters: no sampling, no bootstrap variance. Vanilla ER (G1) draws $256$ samples with replacement, so its replay gradient is a stochastic estimator of this same quantity. The estimator contributor $G_{\text{est}}$ is therefore exactly the gap-depth difference between an estimator-driven step and an exact-gradient step, and $G_{\text{traj}}$ in G4 is the residual once both the estimator and the magnitude asymmetry are removed.

\paragraph{Decomposition shares.} The two first-step contributors act through the instantaneous magnitude asymmetry of the boundary update, so they are read from \texttt{gap\_depth}. The magnitude share ($G_{\text{mag}}$) is the depth drop from G1 to G3, removed by balancing alone; the estimator share ($G_{\text{est}}$) is the further depth drop from G3 to G4, the additional reduction from full data on top of balancing. The trajectory contribution is different in kind: it is not an instantaneous spike but the slow drift back along the moving valley floor of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, a hanging bend rather than a notch. Once both other biases are removed at G4 its residual \emph{depth} is therefore expected to be small, while the trajectory term shows up instead in \texttt{gap\_area\_end} and the recovery time, which integrate the bend that depth misses. We accordingly read $G_{\text{mag}}$ and $G_{\text{est}}$ from depth differences but quantify the trajectory residual ($G_{\text{traj}}$) from the area and recovery at G4. The G2 condition is a sanity check on the estimator share: $\texttt{gap\_depth}(\text{G1}) - \texttt{gap\_depth}(\text{G2})$ should recover $G_{\text{est}}$ along an orthogonal path through the design.

\paragraph{NCL reference.} NCL is included as a path-finding baseline (Section~\ref{subsec:pathfinding_vs_shaping}): it accepts the discontinuity but preconditions each SGD step by the past-task precision matrix.

\paragraph{Prediction.} Gap depth reduces along the path G1 $\rightarrow$ G3 $\rightarrow$ G4 as the two first-step contributors are removed. What remains at G4 is the trajectory residual: small in depth but visible as a bend in \texttt{gap\_area\_end} (the prediction of \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}, Section~\ref{subsec:stability_gap}), and the contributor the $\lambda$-curriculum should additionally suppress.

\subsubsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\lambda$}{lambda}-Curriculum Sweep}
\label{subsec:curriculum}

\paragraph{Aim.} Test the envelope-theorem prediction of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, that a continuous $\lambda$-schedule eliminates the trajectory contribution to the gap, with a transient that shrinks as $O(1/N)$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}), the $\lambda_{\min}$ trade-off, and the momentum cross-cut of RQ2d. Answers RQ2a--RQ2d.

\paragraph{Design.} All seven curriculum conditions (labelled C1--C7) are applied on top of standard ER ($1$k reservoir, no balancing, no NCL) so the curriculum's effect is isolated (Table~\ref{tab:curriculum}). As stated in Section~\ref{subsec:overview}, each is run at both momentum $0.0$ and $0.9$.

The adaptive schedule (C4) is self-paced: it sets $\lambda$ to a clipped exponential moving average (EMA) of the gradient-norm ratio $r := \|g_{\text{replay}}\| / \|g_{\text{new}}\|$ (Table~\ref{tab:curriculum}). At $\theta_0^*$ the replay gradient is near zero by stationarity, so $r$ is near zero and $\lambda$ starts near $0$. As the iterate leaves $\theta_0^*$, $\|g_{\text{replay}}\|$ grows while $\|g_{\text{new}}\|$ shrinks as the model fits $T_1$, so $r$ climbs and $\lambda \to 1$ with no explicit ramp length; the schedule ignores $N$ entirely. The floor $\lambda_{\min}$ (C5--C7) matters only for this adaptive variant: it stops the schedule from sitting at $\lambda \approx 0$ in the first steps, when the EMA estimate of $r$ is still close to zero.

\paragraph{Prediction.} We hypothesize three main outcomes based on our research questions. First, as $N$ increases from 50 to 200, \texttt{gap\_depth} should decrease monotonically, consistent with the $O(1/N)$ bound in Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}. Simultaneously, overall accuracy (ACC) should remain comparable to vanilla Experience Replay (ER). This is because our curriculum preserves the natural step-size adaptation of SGD, rather than artificially rescaling the steps as gradient balancing does (RQ2a). Second, implementing an adaptive schedule should achieve a comparable reduction in gap depth without requiring a fixed value for $N$ (RQ2b). Finally, introducing a small minimum floor, $\lambda_{\min} \in [0.05, 0.10, 0.20]$, should restore early learning velocity on task $T_1$. While this causes a slight increase in gap depth, it yields faster early learning and higher final accuracy, ultimately achieving a better Pareto balance between depth and accuracy than setting $\lambda_{\min} = 0$ (RQ2c).

The contrast between balanced ER (G3) and linear $N{=}200$ (C3) isolates the difference between acting on the magnitude symptom alone and acting on the schedule discontinuity: both should suppress depth, but only the curriculum should preserve ACC.

\paragraph{Momentum (RQ2d).}
\label{subsec:momentum}
Because every condition described above (including the G-series decomposition) is run at both $0.0$ and $0.9$ momentum, this parameter sweep simultaneously tests the two momentum predictions of RQ2d. Momentum acts as a trajectory mechanism. It cannot redirect the deterministic, one-sided first step at a task boundary, but it does dampen the stochastic oscillation as the model tracks the moving optimal path afterward. Consequently, its clearest signature appears in the trajectory metrics, specifically \texttt{gap\_area\_end} and the per-step oscillation amplitude on the $T_0$ curve, rather than in the initial performance spike.

Its effect on \texttt{gap\_depth} depends entirely on what causes the performance drop. Without the curriculum (G1), the depth is determined strictly by the deterministic first-step spike. Therefore, momentum should leave \texttt{gap\_depth} unchanged. The depth for vanilla Experience Replay (ER) at $0.9$ momentum should exactly match the depth at $0.0$ momentum, even though momentum still shrinks the subsequent recovery bend measured by \texttt{gap\_area\_end}.

In contrast, when the curriculum is applied (the C-series), the first step is held near zero, and the deepest drop occurs later on the trajectory bend itself. Dampening this bend should logically reduce both \texttt{gap\_area\_end} and \texttt{gap\_depth}, while also tightening the variance across different random seeds.

Ultimately, the comparison between $\text{G1}$ and $\text{G1}_M$ tests this falsification by predicting a null effect on depth. Meanwhile, the contrast between $\text{C3}$ and $\text{C3}_M$, along with the adaptive $\text{C4}$ condition, measures the exact trajectory residual that momentum successfully removes.

\subsubsection{Long-Sequence rot-MNIST}
\label{subsec:longseq}

\paragraph{Aim.} The two-task design isolates a single transition, which keeps the decomposition clean but leaves two questions open: whether the curriculum still suppresses the gap across several consecutive boundaries, and whether NCL recovers the advantage that \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess} report at the higher task counts where path-finding methods are strongest.

\paragraph{Design.} We use a five-task rot-MNIST sequence with rotations of $[-90, -60, -30, 0, 30]^\circ$. This creates four consecutive transitions, each with a uniform $30^\circ$ step. The network backbone, online learning regime, and dense per-step evaluation remain identical to the two-task block described in Section~\ref{subsec:testbed}.

Momentum is fixed at $0.9$ for this phase. We do not test multiple momentum values here because the earlier C-series and the G1 versus G1$_M$ contrast already characterize the momentum trade-off during a single transition, making further comparison redundant.

As outlined in Table~\ref{tab:longseq}, we evaluate four experimental conditions: vanilla ER, tuned NCL, and the adaptive curriculum at two different floor values ($\lambda_{\min} \in \{0, 0.10\}$). Each condition is run across five random seeds. Finally, the gap depth and accuracy metrics are calculated by averaging the results across all four task transitions.

\paragraph{Prediction.} The adaptive curriculum should keep a reduced gap depth across all four transitions at no accuracy cost relative to vanilla ER. NCL may reach a lower depth at this higher task count, consistent with \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}, but is expected to carry the accuracy cost it shows at a single transition.

\subsubsection{Generalisation: CIFAR-10}
\label{subsec:cifar}

\paragraph{Aim.} This experiment tests the generalization of our rot-MNIST findings by applying them to a stronger task shift and a deeper, non-linear architecture. Specifically, we investigate whether our theoretical predictions hold in a regime where the assumptions underlying the envelope-theorem bound begin to weaken (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). We use the two-task Gaussian-noise domain shift described in Section~\ref{subsec:testbed} as the primary evaluation dataset.

\paragraph{Training protocol.} We use the CIFAR-adapted ResNet-18 detailed in Table~\ref{tab:resnet}. Because this deeper architecture requires more than a single pass to converge before a task transition, we train for ten epochs per task. The setup uses a batch size of $256$, an SGD learning rate of $0.1$, and a replay buffer of $5{,}000$ samples. To focus entirely on generalization, we fix momentum at $0.9$, which was the better-performing configuration from the rot-MNIST experiments, rather than repeating the full parameter sweep from Section~\ref{subsec:overview}. We follow the evaluation scheme outlined in Section~\ref{subsubsec:eval_protocol}, recording metrics at every step for the first $50$ steps after the transition, and every $10$ steps thereafter.

\paragraph{Headline conditions.} To test how robustly our method generalizes, we evaluate six distinct conditions. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:cifar}, we compare three methods: vanilla Experience Replay (ER), NCL, and our adaptive curriculum set to $\lambda_{\min}=0.20$. We omit the linear curriculum for CIFAR and instead carry over only this specific adaptive variant, as it yielded the best final accuracy in the prior rot-MNIST sweep. Similarly, we pin the NCL hyperparameters to the optimal values found in the earlier tuning phase (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Furthermore, we cross each of these three methods with both BatchNorm and GroupNorm. This normalization crossover is deliberate because it directly impacts post-switch recovery dynamics. The input shift disrupts the running statistics of BatchNorm, requiring it to re-adapt during the initial steps of the new task. GroupNorm, conversely, relies on no cross-batch statistics. Comparing the two allows us to isolate the performance drop caused purely by optimization dynamics, which our curriculum actively targets, from the drop caused by disrupted normalization statistics.

The curriculum should also keep beating its vanilla control on the novel corruptions of the generalisation block. The quantitative $O(1/N)$ scaling of the bound is not expected to hold tightly at this depth; deviations are reported and discussed alongside the results.
