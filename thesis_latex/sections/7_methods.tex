\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

This section describes the empirical testbeds (Section~\ref{subsec:setup}), the metrics that summarise each run (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}), and the experimental blocks (Section~\ref{subsec:overview}) that answer the research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research_questions}. All code, experiment configurations, and analysis scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/Arthurreuss/ValleyWalk}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

\subsubsection{Testbeds}
\label{subsec:testbed}

Two testbeds of different scale carry the study. A small, fast one (rotated MNIST on a two-layer MLP) runs the dense mechanistic sweeps: the driver block, the curriculum sweep, the asymmetric-PER damping sweep, and the momentum cross. A larger one (domain-shift CIFAR-10 on a CIFAR-adapted ResNet-18) tests whether the findings survive a deeper backbone and a stronger task shift, the regime where the local condition behind the bound of Section~\ref{subsec:reference} weakens. Both hold the label space and task structure fixed and shift only the input distribution, and both use two tasks in their main blocks, so there is exactly one transition and one instance of the stability gap; a five-task rot-MNIST sequence and a three-task CIFAR sequence serve as multi-boundary checks.

On rot-MNIST, following \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning}, $T_0$ presents the digits at $-90^\circ$ and $T_1$ applies a further $90^\circ$ rotation, each task using the standard $60{,}000$ training and $10{,}000$ test images. On CIFAR-10, $T_0$ is clean and $T_1$ applies Gaussian-noise corruption at severity $3$ \citep{hendrycks2019robustness}; the ten classes are shared, so only the input distribution shifts. A three-task variant ($[\text{none}, \text{Gaussian}, \text{shot}]$) and two alternative corruptions appear in the generalisation block. The rot-MNIST MLP is cheap enough to sweep across many conditions and seeds and is where the local condition of Section~\ref{subsec:reference} is most plausible; the ResNet-18 is the deeper, more realistic counterpart. rot-MNIST runs online (one epoch per task); the ResNet needs several passes to converge before the transition, so the CIFAR block uses ten epochs per task. Optimiser, learning rate, and batch size are common across benchmarks and identical across every condition of a block; no condition retunes the learning rate, so all comparisons run at matched step size (Section~\ref{subsec:gates}), and the step size's own contribution is measured by the dedicated warm-up conditions of Section~\ref{subsec:drivers}. Full backbone and protocol specifications are in Appendix~\ref{app:impl_models} (Tables~\ref{tab:mlp}--\ref{tab:training}).

Throughout training every task's test accuracy is evaluated every $10$ steps; at each transition the cadence rises to every step, so the dip is recorded at full resolution (the whole online epoch on rot-MNIST, a $250$-step window on CIFAR). A stability-gap tracker snapshots each past task's pre-switch accuracy and records the resulting (step, accuracy) series. Because the coarse pre-transition cadence leaves a short interval between the last pre-switch evaluation and the first post-update one, the per-step figures hold the last pre-switch level up to the boundary rather than interpolating across that interval; the past task is still training and at its plateau there, so the alternative would draw the drop as beginning before the switch. All reported metrics are computed from the logged samples themselves and are unaffected by this plotting convention. For the driver block only, a gradient tracker additionally records the fidelity of the replay-buffer gradient against the exact past-task gradient, at the same cadence; each such step costs a full pass over the past-task training set, which is why it is not run elsewhere.

\subsubsection{Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Each metric is reported as the seed mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation; complete definitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:metric_defs}. The gap definition of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, the transient excess below the level a task settles at, gives two primary metrics. With $a_j^{\text{pre}}$ the pre-switch baseline on past task $j$ and $a_j(t)$ its accuracy at step $t$, \texttt{gap\_depth} is the maximum instantaneous excursion $\max_j (a_j^{\text{pre}} - \min_t a_j(t))$, which Section~\ref{subsec:trajectory_problem} predicts to be overshoot-dominated. The second, \texttt{gap\_area\_end}, integrates the shortfall below the settled level: with reference $b_j = \min(a_j^{\text{pre}}, \bar{a}_j)$, where $\bar{a}_j$ is the accuracy the task eventually reaches, it sums $\max(0, b_j - a_j(t))$ over the new task's training steps and across past tasks. Anchoring to the recovered level implements the split of Section~\ref{subsec:reference}: a permanent step down to a lower plateau contributes to the standard forgetting metrics and near-zero to the area, while a true dip-and-recover registers in full. One consequence, revisited when reading the tables, is that two methods settling at different levels are measured against different lines, so a better-recovering method can carry the \emph{larger} area; a positive area marks transient disruption, not net cost, and we read it with final accuracy. The arc of Section~\ref{subsec:trajectory_problem} and the slow part of the noisy recovery live here; sharp noise-driven drops register in depth instead. A third metric, \texttt{recovery\_steps}, and the gradient-fidelity diagnostics are defined in Appendix~\ref{app:metric_defs}.

To place the gap in the standard frame we report the continual-evaluation metrics of \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} from the task-boundary accuracy matrix: average final accuracy (ACC), which checks that a gate lowers the gap at preserved accuracy; worst-case retention (min-ACC, WC-ACC); and short-horizon plasticity ($\mathrm{WP}_{100}$). FORG and the windowed $\mathrm{WF}_w/\mathrm{WP}_w$ family are logged for completeness. Conditions are compared with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on seed-paired differences, preferred over the $t$-test because five paired seeds cannot support a normality assumption on bounded metrics: the gap metrics floor at zero, where the strongest conditions cluster, and accuracy sits near its ceiling, so the seed differences are skewed rather than Gaussian. With five paired seeds the smallest two-sided $p$-value is $0.0625$, reached when all five differences share a sign; we read $p = 0.0625$ as complete directional agreement and let effect sizes separate large results from marginal ones.

\subsection{Experiments}
\label{subsec:overview}

Each block maps to one or more research questions and tests the corresponding predictions of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}. All conditions use five seeds and the protocol above unless noted.


\subsubsection{Step and Field Interventions (RQ1)}
\label{subsec:drivers}

The driver block answers the overshoot side of RQ1 with interventions that act either on the \emph{step}, the Level-Two machinery, or on the \emph{boundary field}, the Level-One terrain, each switched on by construction. The complementary path-side intervention is the curriculum, whose full-buffer diagnostic below completes the RQ1 cross. Table~\ref{tab:drivers} lists the conditions.

Two conditions intervene on the step. The full-data buffer (D2, D4) replaces vanilla ER's noisy $256$-sample replay gradient with the exact empirical past-task gradient, removing the estimator variance (mechanics in Appendix~\ref{app:impl_er}). The learning-rate warm-up (M1--M3) scales the learning rate by $\min((t-1)/N,\, 1)$ over the first $N$ steps of each new task, shortening the first discrete updates while leaving the update direction untouched; if the acute driver is the step size meeting the boundary field (Section~\ref{subsec:trajectory_problem}), this alone should cut the drop. Its ramp lengths are matched to the linear curriculum's ($N \in \{50, 100, 200\}$), so the same runs double as the symmetric control for RQ2a: the warm-up slows the new-task gradient \emph{and} the restoring gradient equally, where the curriculum scales only the new-task loss.

One condition intervenes on the field. Balanced-direction ER (D3, D4) removes the boundary imbalance from the update direction while leaving the step length untouched: the update steps along the equal-magnitude mix $\hat{g}_{\text{new}} + \hat{g}_{\text{rep}}$, rescaled to the length $\lVert g_{\text{new}} + g_{\text{rep}} \rVert$ that vanilla ER would have stepped at the same parameters. The two levels make opposite predictions for it. If the imbalance itself drives the drop, remixing should cut depth. If the imbalance is Level-One terrain, remixing at full boundary step length cannot remove the drop and may deepen it: at $\theta_0^*$ (the converged $T_0$ optimum, $\theta_A^*$ of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}) the replay gradient vanishes by stationarity, so its normalised direction carries no information, and the mix hands that direction half of a full-length step. An earlier version of this block instead normalised the joint update to unit length, which fixes the step length at $\eta$ and so confounds the remix with a step-size intervention; those conditions and their results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:old_balanced}.

Because the interventions interact, we read the conditions as interventions, not an additive decomposition: depth differences measure how much of the drop each lever carries in that context, while the area and per-step shape carry the Level-One signal: with the replay estimate exact (D2, D4), a dip-and-recover bend that remains cannot come from the variance driver, the arc's fingerprint.

\subsubsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\lambda$}{lambda}-Curriculum Sweep (RQ2, RQ1)}
\label{subsec:curriculum}

The curriculum sweep tests the scalar feedback gate one ingredient at a time: the $O(1/N)$ prediction of Equation~\eqref{eq:bound} (RQ2a), the adaptive schedule (RQ2b), and the $\lambda_{\min}$ floor (RQ2c); a full-buffer diagnostic (C8) then returns to RQ1, testing the claim that the curriculum removes the arc and leaves only stochastic overshoot. The linear ramps C1--C3 are compared per seed against the learning-rate warm-up M1--M3 of Table~\ref{tab:drivers} at matched $N$: the warm-up shortens every step symmetrically, so any margin the curriculum holds over it isolates what the one-sided gate adds beyond ``small steps early'' (RQ2a). All conditions run on top of standard ER ($1$k reservoir, unbalanced gradients), so the schedule is the only changed variable (Table~\ref{tab:curriculum}). The adaptive schedule (C4) is the feedback variant of Section~\ref{subsec:gates}, a clipped exponential moving average of the gradient-norm ratio; it starts near $0$ at $\theta_0^*$ by stationarity and self-terminates when the gradients rebalance, with no ramp length to choose (mechanics in Appendix~\ref{app:impl_er}). The floor $\lambda_{\min}$ (C5--C7) keeps the schedule off $\lambda \approx 0$ during the first steps and buys back early plasticity.

C8 combines the practical configuration (C7) with the full $60$k buffer of D2/D4, fixing the path \emph{and} switching the variance driver off at source. It is a double probe: if C7's remaining momentum-$0$ transient is buffer noise, C8 should show no gap even without momentum; and since the full buffer leaves no variance for momentum to reduce, whatever momentum still improves on top of C8 must come from its other role, accelerating new-task learning. C8 breaks the limited-memory premise and is diagnostic only; C7 remains the practical configuration.

\subsubsection{Momentum Cross (RQ2d, RQ3)}
\label{subsec:momentum}

Every rot-MNIST condition of the driver D-series, the warm-up M-series, and the curriculum C-series, and the asymmetric-PER sweep legs P1--P2 at the settings noted in Table~\ref{tab:per}, is run at momentum $0.0$ and $0.9$; momentum-on runs carry an $M$ subscript (e.g.\ $\text{D1}_M$). The cross tests the composability prediction of Section~\ref{subsec:gates}: whether momentum helps or hurts a method's gap should depend on where the method attenuates the push. A five-task rot-MNIST sequence (rotations $[0,30,60,90,120]^\circ$, four transitions, five seeds) repeats the three-way comparison of vanilla ER, the practical curriculum, and asymmetric PER at both momentum settings, as a multi-boundary check (Appendix~\ref{app:longseq_full}).

\subsubsection{Asymmetric-PER \texorpdfstring{$\delta$}{delta} Sweep (RQ3)}
\label{subsec:per_asym}

The damping sweep tests the directional feedforward gate: does the gap shrink as the filter strengthens, at what plasticity cost, and where does the gate fail? Asymmetric PER implements Equation~\eqref{eq:asym_per} \citep{amari1998natural}; along an eigendirection of replay curvature $\rho$ the gain is $\delta/(\rho+\delta)$, so $\delta \to \infty$ recovers plain ER and $\delta \to 0$ concentrates the push in replay-flat directions, with the restoring gradient $g_{\text{rep}}$ at full strength throughout (Section~\ref{subsec:gates}). The push is applied matrix-free by conjugate gradients over Fisher-vector products, and the Fisher is re-estimated at every step from the current parameters on a sampled replay batch (Appendix~\ref{app:impl_per}). When momentum is enabled, it acts downstream of the gate: the filtered direction enters the velocity accumulator, and the accumulated velocity is applied unfiltered; preconditioning the velocity instead is a distinct design, discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:disc_outlook}.

Table~\ref{tab:per} lays out the sweep: $\delta \in \{1.0, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03\}$ crossed with two buffer legs, each at both momentum settings. The \emph{standard} leg ($1$k reservoir) matches the curriculum's practical regime, while the \emph{full-buffer} leg makes the replay gradient exact, switching off the variance driver as in D2/D4, so the remaining area measures the deterministic arc and the sweep reads out directly whether the gate shrinks the arc itself. Two controls guard the reading: a solver control reruns the strongest filter at $60$ CG iterations to separate gate properties from an under-converged solve (Appendix~\ref{app:impl_per}), and a symmetric control preconditions the summed gradient by the same replay Fisher, isolating the effect of leaving the restoring gradient untouched (Appendix~\ref{app:per_sym}).

\subsubsection{Generalisation: CIFAR-10 (RQ4)}
\label{subsec:cifar}

The CIFAR-10 block tests whether both gates and the momentum signatures carry to a deeper backbone and a stronger task shift. All CIFAR conditions use the ResNet-18 protocol of Table~\ref{tab:training} (ten epochs per task, buffer $5{,}000$). The headline conditions run at momentum $0.9$ (Table~\ref{tab:cifar}, top), each method crossed with BatchNorm and GroupNorm because the two respond differently at the switch: the input shift disrupts BatchNorm's running statistics, which must re-adapt during the first steps, while GroupNorm uses no cross-batch statistics, so its transient reflects optimisation dynamics alone. The curriculum runs as the adaptive schedule at $\lambda_{\min}=0.20$; asymmetric PER runs at $\delta=0.1$, a strong rot-MNIST setting. Both carry their rot-MNIST configurations without a CIFAR-specific sweep, so they meet the deeper backbone on equal footing. A momentum cross (Table~\ref{tab:cifar}, bottom) adds momentum-$0$ runs of all three methods at GroupNorm, repeating the three-way composability test where the transient is free of normalisation-statistics effects; because momentum also drives convergence on this backbone, accuracy is compared within a momentum setting. A further generalisation block pairs the practical curriculum against a paired vanilla-ER control on shifts it was never tuned on (a two-task shot-noise shift, a two-task contrast shift, and a three-task $[\text{none}, \text{Gaussian}, \text{shot}]$ sequence, each at both normalisations over three seeds) to test whether the result depends on the particular corruption or on having a single transition.
