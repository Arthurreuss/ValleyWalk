\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deep neural networks learn powerful representations from static, independent, and identically distributed data, but a vulnerability appears as soon as training becomes sequential: the network overwrites previously acquired knowledge to accommodate new data. This phenomenon, catastrophic forgetting \cite{mccloskey:catastrophic, ratclif1990catastrophicforgetting}, is a major hurdle in machine learning. While retraining from scratch solves the issue, it is simply too expensive for systems that need to adapt dynamically. Continual learning (CL) studies how to learn from a sequence of tasks without that cost, and its core difficulty is the stability--plasticity dilemma: the model must stay plastic enough to learn new distributions while staying stable enough to preserve old ones \cite{Mermillod2013Stability-Plasticity}.

Several families of techniques address forgetting: replay, parameter and functional regularisation, optimisation-based methods, context-dependent processing, and template-based classification \cite{van_de_Ven_2025}. These methods reduce long-term forgetting, but until recently they have been evaluated only at task boundaries or at coarse intervals, which hides what happens within a task. Evaluating the network continuously exposes a failure these protocols miss. \citet{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning} did exactly this and identified the \textit{Stability Gap}, a transient but severe collapse in past-task accuracy that occurs immediately upon the introduction of a new task.

This matters for two reasons. Because deployed continual-learning systems are queried throughout training, a model caught mid-transition will commit errors that its final checkpoint never would. Secondly, since the model eventually recovers, past knowledge is disrupted rather than lost. Identifying this disruptive mechanism is essential to close the gap efficiently and avoid the computational waste of relearning.

The discovery of the gap raised an immediate question: could it be removed by better approximating the joint loss over all tasks? \citet{hess2024complementaryperspectivescontinuallearning} showed that the gap persists even under incremental joint training, where the model has access to all data from all tasks at every step. A perfect objective still produces transient forgetting, so the problem lies not in \emph{what} is optimised but in \emph{how} the optimisation proceeds.

To see why the trajectory itself produces part of the gap, consider the geometry of sequential optimisation. Figure~\ref{fig:loss_landscape_visualization} visualises a transition from a learned task (Task A) to a new task (Task B), a conceptual two-dimensional reduction of a high-dimensional surface that nonetheless captures the dynamics at play. Our depiction of the transition geometry borrows the loss-landscape lens of \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}, who visualise sequential optimisation in this way to motivate curvature-aware updates; we adopt their geometric framing while drawing the opposite conclusion about how to respond to it (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). The network starts at the converged minimum $A$ of Task A and must reach the joint minimum $A\cap B$. Running stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on the joint loss from $A$ follows the green curve: pulled by the steepest gradients of the joint landscape, the trajectory bows outward, leaving Task A's basin and crossing a region of higher Task-A error before converging at $A\cap B$. The dip on Task A along this arc is part of the stability gap.

This trajectory is the consequence of a discontinuity at the task boundary. While Task A is learned the optimiser descends the replay loss alone, the red basin in Figure~\ref{fig:loss_landscape_visualization}. The moment Task B begins, the objective switches in a single step to the sum of the replay and current-task losses, the purple joint basin. Its minimum at $A\cap B$ replaces the red one from one step to the next. The parameters do not move during this switch, yet the minimum they are chasing jumps from $\theta_A^*$ to $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$. We call this \emph{landscape teleportation}: from the optimiser's point of view the surface it was descending disappears and is replaced by another whose minimum lies elsewhere.

While the trajectory effect explains the gradual, prolonged bowing of the optimisation path, it does not account for the severe, nearly instantaneous collapse in past-task accuracy. This sudden drop is driven instead by a \textbf{magnitude imbalance} during the very first updates. At $\theta_A^*$, the replay gradient is near zero because the optimiser has converged on Task A, while the new-task gradient is large because Task B is completely unlearned. The summed update is therefore dominated by the new-task gradient, causing SGD to take a massive, unopposed step directly out of Task A's basin. Thus, while the trajectory effect curves the overall descent over time, this magnitude asymmetry is responsible for the precipitous initial dip \citep{delange2023continualevaluationlifelonglearning}. Finally, a finite replay buffer introduces a third, smaller error: it estimates the past-task gradient from a handful of stored samples rather than from all past data, creating a noisy replay signal. Trajectory effect, magnitude asymmetry, and estimator variance are the three distinct contributors to the gap; Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition} makes their decomposition precise and the experiments measure each in isolation.

Existing methods take this discontinuity as given. They accept that the objective jumps at the task boundary and instead make the optimiser robust to the jump, constraining each SGD update with information about the past tasks, their gradients or local curvature, so that SGD avoids directions in which the replay loss rises sharply \cite{lopezpaz2022gradientepisodicmemorycontinual, chaudhry2019efficientlifelonglearningagem, Kirkpatrick_2017, kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess}. We group these approaches under the term \emph{path-finding}: while effective, they navigate the broken landscape rather than fixing it.

This thesis takes the opposite route. Rather than steer the optimiser carefully across a discontinuity, we propose removing the discontinuity itself—an approach we call \emph{landscape-shaping}. We instantiate this via the \textbf{$\lambda$-curriculum}: instead of switching abruptly from the replay loss to the joint loss, the curriculum interpolates the two by linearly ramping the new-task weight from $0$ to $1$ over the first $N$ steps of the transition,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        L_\lambda(t) & = L_{\text{replay}} + \lambda(t)\, L_{\text{current}}, \\
        \lambda(t)   & = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{N}, 0, 1\right)\!.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:lambda_curriculum}
\end{equation}
By interpolating the objective functions, the corresponding optimum $\Theta^*(\lambda)$ traces a continuous path in parameter space from $\theta_A^*$ (at $\lambda=0$) to $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$ (at $\lambda=1$). The model merely tracks this moving valley instead of being teleported across the landscape (fourth panel of Figure~\ref{fig:loss_landscape_visualization}). The existence of such traversable, low-loss valleys between optima is supported by the literature on mode connectivity \citep{draxler2019essentiallybarriersneuralnetwork, garipov2018losssurfacesmodeconnectivity}. However, while those studies retroactively search for paths between pre-computed optima, the $\lambda$-curriculum dynamically carves one out, ensuring the optimiser stays within a low-loss corridor and never has to cross a high-loss ridge. Because the target optimum moves gradually, the curriculum neutralizes two of the three gap contributors at once. It eliminates the trajectory effect, since the descent path no longer bows away from Task A's basin, and it tempers the magnitude imbalance, since the new-task gradient is introduced smoothly rather than dominating the first step. Only the third contributor, the variance of the finite-buffer estimator, is independent of the schedule and left untouched. Treating the task-boundary discontinuity as the root cause to be eliminated is the central contribution of this thesis; Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes this argument precise.

Beyond the basic linear ramp, we develop three refinements, each targeting a limitation of the schedule: an \textbf{adaptive schedule} that removes the need to choose the ramp length $N$, a \textbf{$\lambda_{\min}$ floor} that prevents the adaptive schedule from stalling progress on the new task, and \textbf{momentum} that suppresses the per-step accuracy oscillation. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes how each is constructed and why.

Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews continual learning, Experience Replay, the path-finding methods we compare against, the stability-gap literature, and two lines of work, blurry task boundaries and mode connectivity, that motivate the curriculum. Section~\ref{sec:motivation} then motivates the thesis's landscape-shaping approach by decomposing the gap into three contributors and contrasting it with traditional path-finding. Section~\ref{sec:theory} proves that a continuous linear $\lambda$-ramp removes the trajectory contributor, with a residual of order $1/N$ in the ramp length. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_approach} turns the decomposition and the bound into falsifiable predictions, organised as the research questions, and Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the experimental testbed, the metrics, and the conditions used to test each prediction. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the experiments on rotated MNIST and a deeper CIFAR backbone, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to the central claim, weighs the evidence against it, and notes the limitations.
