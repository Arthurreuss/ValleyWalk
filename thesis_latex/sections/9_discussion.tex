\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results let us return to the claim the thesis set out to defend: that in its
dominant components the stability gap is a symptom of the task-boundary
discontinuity, and that removing the discontinuity is a better intervention than
navigating it. The decomposition supports the first half of the claim, the
curriculum-and-momentum results the second. What the numbers also force is a
sharper reading of what ``the gap'' is --- not one quantity but two, a depth and
a hanging tail, that the theory predicts should respond to different parts of the
fix and that come apart most clearly on the deeper backbone.

\subsection{The boundary is the cause, and it has two faces}
\label{subsec:disc_cause}

The decomposition (RQ1) places roughly two-thirds of the rot-MNIST gap in the
first-step magnitude asymmetry and a further fifth in estimator and trajectory
terms, with the trajectory residual surviving exact gradients and unit-norm
balancing. This is the pattern the discontinuity account predicts: the part of
the gap that vanishes when the new-task gradient is introduced gradually
($G_{\text{mag}}$, $G_{\text{traj}}$) is the part tied to the boundary, and the
part that does not ($G_{\text{est}}$) is the buffer's, independent of the
schedule. That the trajectory residual is small on the MLP is not evidence
against it; it is what Equation~\eqref{eq:bound} expects on a network that
reconverges in a few steps, where the $O(1/N)$ tail has little time to
accumulate.

The residual matters because it is the half of the gap that the depth headline
hides. Depth measures the instantaneous notch the one-sided first step cuts;
\texttt{gap\_area\_end} measures the bend that follows, the cost the transient carries
before the curve reconverges. The G4 condition isolates this directly: with the
magnitude spike removed its depth is only $1.9$ pp, yet a visible bend remains
--- the trajectory residual showing up as a hanging curve rather than a spike,
exactly the shape the theory attaches to the $1/N$ term. On rot-MNIST this bend
is cheap. On the CIFAR ResNet, which reconverges over hundreds of steps rather
than a few, the same transient stays open long after the initial notch, so most
of the accuracy the gap costs comes from the prolonged recovery rather than the
depth of the dip --- and that recovery cost is where the curriculum's margin over
the baselines is largest.
The depth-versus-area distinction is therefore not bookkeeping: it is the axis
along which the method's value scales with how slowly a network recovers, and a
depth-only reading would have understated the result on exactly the architectures
where it counts.

The same distinction guards against a false negative in the other direction. NCL
reaches a low depth on rot-MNIST but its curve steps to a lower plateau and stays
there, which a gap metric referenced to the pre-task accuracy would score as
severe instability. Referenced instead to the level each task recovers to, that
shortfall is correctly read as permanent forgetting --- the same loss ACC and
FORG already report --- and not as a transient the curriculum could close. The
gap and forgetting are different failures, and keeping them apart is what lets
the decomposition attribute the gap to the boundary rather than to capacity loss.

\subsection{Why shaping beats path-finding}
\label{subsec:disc_shaping}

The cleanest separation in the study is the contrast between NCL and the
curriculum on the deeper backbone. The two interventions target the same
discontinuity from opposite sides: NCL accepts the jump and preconditions each
update with past-task curvature (path-finding); the curriculum removes the jump
and leaves the geometry of each step to SGD (landscape-shaping). On rot-MNIST
both lower depth, so the two are hard to tell apart. On CIFAR they diverge
sharply --- NCL's preconditioner makes the gap \emph{larger} than vanilla ER
under batch norm, the opposite of its rot-MNIST behaviour, while the curriculum's
benefit holds across both normalisations and both backbones.

This is the failure mode the motivation anticipated for path-finding
(Section~\ref{subsec:pathfinding_vs_shaping}). A preconditioner rescales the
gradient, and on a deeper network the curvature estimate it depends on is harder
to get right and its rescaling interferes with the step-size adaptation SGD
relies on; when the estimate is off, steering across the discontinuity can steer
badly. The curriculum changes only a scalar loss weight and leaves the step size
untouched, so it has nothing analogous to mis-estimate. The result is the
practical case for the thesis's methodological stance: an intervention that
removes the problematic structure degrades more gracefully with depth than one
that builds machinery to cope with it. We do not claim path-finding fails in
general --- NCL reaches the lowest depth of any method on the long rot-MNIST
sequence, consistent with \citet{kao2021naturalcontinuallearningsuccess} --- only
that it pays an accuracy cost the curriculum does not and that its advantage did
not survive the move to a deeper backbone.

\subsection{Two mechanisms, two parts of the fix}
\label{subsec:disc_momentum}

The momentum cross (RQ2d) is the most direct test of the theory's mechanism, and
it confirms a falsifiable null: momentum alone, without the curriculum, leaves
the gap essentially intact, with seeds disagreeing on even the sign of the small
change. This is expected, because the first step at the boundary is
deterministic --- the new-task gradient against a near-zero replay gradient ---
and averaging cannot soften a step that has no stochastic component to average.
Momentum becomes decisive only once the curriculum has made the optimum path
continuous, at which point the residual gap is the stochastic oscillation around
that path and momentum averages it away, collapsing both the depth and its
seed-to-seed variance.

Read against Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}, the curriculum and momentum address
different terms. The curriculum lengthens the ramp, which shrinks the
deterministic curvature spike ($1/N^2$, the depth) and the trajectory lag ($1/N$,
the area) but cannot remove the per-step jitter the continuous-limit argument
abstracts away. Momentum removes that jitter but does nothing to the
deterministic step. Neither alone closes the gap to a few points at matched
accuracy; together they do. The $\lambda_{\min}$ floor then trades a small slice
of depth back for early plasticity, and only becomes a clean Pareto knob once
momentum has suppressed the deterministic step the floor cannot touch --- which
is why it barely registered at momentum $0$ and tuned a real trade-off at
momentum $0.9$. The three refinements are not interchangeable knobs; each acts on
a part of the gap the others leave alone, in the order the theory predicts.

\subsection{Relation to blurry task boundaries}
\label{subsec:disc_blurry}

The curriculum and the blurry-boundary stream of Section~\ref{subsec:blurry}
descend the same moving mixture of old- and new-task gradients, but they differ
in what they hold fixed, and that difference is what makes the curriculum the
cleaner test of the mechanism. A blurry stream changes the data distribution and
the loss composition together: as $T_1$ samples enter the batch the gradient
reweights, but the set of examples the model trains on also changes, so a
reduction in forgetting cannot be attributed to the gradual reweighting alone. A
blurry stream also carries no lasting retention pressure --- once the transition
completes and the batch is entirely $T_1$, the old-task data leaves the stream
and nothing anchors the model to the old optimum, so forgetting can resume. The
$\lambda$-curriculum leaves data sampling and buffer contents untouched, varies
only the weight on the current-task loss, and retains an explicit replay term
after the new task has fully ramped in. It therefore isolates the reweighting as
the one variable responsible for any change in the gap and sustains the retention
signal a blurry stream loses. Our result supports the blurry-boundary finding
that softer transitions forget less \citep{koh2022onlinecontinuallearningclass},
while separating the causal variables that line of work leaves entangled.

The result also rests on the low-loss corridor that mode connectivity establishes
(Section~\ref{subsec:lmc}). The curriculum can only keep the
optimiser on a continuous path from $\theta_A^*$ to $\theta_{\text{joint}}^*$ if
such a path exists; \citet{mirzadeh2020linearmodeconnectivitymultitask} show that
in stable training regimes the sequential and joint solutions lie in the same
linearly connected basin. The curriculum's job is to hold the iterate on that
corridor rather than let it bow away, and the trajectory residual it leaves is
the small lag with which a finite-rate ramp tracks the corridor's moving floor.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:disc_limits}

Three limits bound these claims. The first is statistical: five seeds put a floor
of $p = 0.0625$ on the two-sided Wilcoxon test, so we lean on effect sizes and
seed-paired direction rather than a $0.05$ threshold the design cannot reach. The
effects we rest on --- the order-of-magnitude depth reductions on CIFAR, the
variance collapse under momentum --- are large relative to the seed spread, but
the smaller ones, including the $\lambda_{\min}$ trade-offs, are read as
directional consistency, not significance.

The second is the accuracy drift of the linear curriculum at momentum $0$, where
lengthening the ramp lowers depth but costs several points of final accuracy. The
prediction that the curriculum matches vanilla accuracy holds in the
momentum-on regime that the method ships in, but not at momentum $0$, where a
slow ramp holds the new task back long enough to leave a mark on final accuracy.
The method's accuracy-neutrality is therefore a property of the full
curriculum-plus-momentum configuration, not of the ramp alone.

The third is the three-task group-norm sequence, where the adaptive schedule
underperformed its vanilla control with high seed variance, an anomaly absent
from the batch-norm variant of the same sequence. We read this as the adaptive
gradient-ratio signal interacting poorly with several closely spaced group-norm
transitions rather than a single isolated one, and flag it as a boundary of the
method's reliability. More broadly, the headline analysis is built on a
single-transition, two-task design chosen to isolate one boundary cleanly; the
long-sequence checks suggest the property carries, but a systematic study across
many boundaries, longer task sequences, and class-incremental rather than
domain-incremental shifts is left open.

\subsection{Implications and outlook}
\label{subsec:disc_outlook}

The practical reading is narrow but concrete. For continual-learning systems that
are queried during training rather than only at task boundaries, the cost of the
gap is the accuracy lost while the transient is open, and that cost grows with
how slowly the network reconverges. The curriculum addresses exactly this cost,
at almost no final-accuracy penalty and at negligible overhead --- a single
changing scalar, no stored curvature, no gradient projection --- which makes it
cheap to add on top of an existing replay pipeline.

Two directions follow from the limitations. The first is the multi-transition
regime where the adaptive schedule showed strain: an adaptive signal robust to
closely spaced boundaries, perhaps one that conditions on a boundary detector
rather than on the raw gradient ratio, would extend the method past its current
reliable range. The second is the scope of the bound itself, which is stated for
the trajectory contributor and weakens once the new task's curvature overpowers
the old (Section~\ref{subsec:partA_summary}); characterising where that crossover
sits, and whether the magnitude tempering the curriculum also provides admits a
guarantee of its own, would narrow the distance between what the theory proves
about the trajectory term and what the experiments show the full method does.
