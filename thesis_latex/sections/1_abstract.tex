\begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    Replay-based continual learning is usually judged by the accuracy it reaches \emph{after} each task. Continual evaluation exposes a failure this view hides: the \emph{stability gap}, a sharp but transient drop in past-task accuracy at the start of every new task. The gap persists even when the optimiser descends the exact joint loss, so its cause lies in the trajectory, not the objective. This thesis explains the gap on two levels. A reference path exists along which the past-task loss rises monotonically to its unavoidable joint-optimum level; everything above that level is avoidable in principle. Steepest descent after an abrupt task switch leaves this path, bowing out of the old task's basin and returning from outside (level one, the trajectory problem), and real optimisers additionally overshoot whatever path they follow, pushed by an imbalanced first step and finite-buffer gradient noise (level two, the overshoot problem). Written in a unified update rule, replay methods that modify the update counter both problems with the same tool: a \emph{gate} on the new-task gradient, with the replay gradient restoring at full strength. We build and compare the two pure gate designs. The \emph{$\lambda$-curriculum}, a scalar feedback gate, ramps the new-task loss weight up from zero so the optimiser tracks the moving valley floor of the reference path; \emph{asymmetric preconditioned experience replay}, a directional feedforward gate, filters the new-task gradient through past-task curvature, braking it only where the past task is steep. On rotated MNIST the two levels separate cleanly: removing the overshoot drivers leaves the arc, fixing the path leaves the overshoot, and doing both removes the gap while the permanent stability--plasticity cost remains. With momentum the curriculum cuts the gap to a few points of depth at vanilla-ER accuracy. The directional gate lowers the gap monotonically as its filter strengthens, at no plasticity cost but far higher compute; on an exact-gradient diagnostic its strongest setting fails late and abruptly, consistent with blind spots of its sampled curvature metric, and momentum re-inflates its per-step filter. Which gate wins is therefore set by the momentum regime: without momentum the directional gate is better on both stability and accuracy; with momentum, standard in practice, the scalar gate takes over. On CIFAR-10 with a ResNet-18 the curriculum cuts gap depth several-fold below vanilla experience replay at matched final accuracy and negligible compute cost, under two normalisation schemes and on corruptions it was never tuned on.
\end{abstract}
