\begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    Replay-based continual learning is usually judged by the accuracy it reaches \emph{after} each task. Continual evaluation exposes what this view hides: the \emph{stability gap}, a sharp but transient drop in past-task accuracy at the start of every new task. The gap persists even when the optimiser descends the exact joint loss, so at least part of its cause lies in the optimisation trajectory rather than the objective. This thesis explains the gap on two levels. The \emph{intended trajectory}, the path exact gradient descent would take, bows out of the old task's basin before returning; and the followed path overshoots the intended one through everything that vanishes with the step size: discrete updates, momentum, and gradient noise. Written in a unified update rule, replay methods counter both levels with a single tool, a \emph{gate} on the new-task gradient with the replay gradient restoring at full strength, and we build and compare the two pure gate designs: an adaptive $\lambda$-curriculum (scalar, feedback) and asymmetric Fisher-preconditioned replay (directional, feedforward). On rotated MNIST, targeted interventions pull the levels apart. Shortening the first steps with a learning-rate warm-up removes the sharp boundary drop; remixing the boundary gradients to equal magnitude at matched step size deepens it instead; and an exact replay gradient leaves a smooth arc that no repair of the update removes. The curriculum matches the warm-up without momentum but beats it decisively with momentum, cuts the gap to a few points of depth at vanilla-ER accuracy, and carries to CIFAR-10 on a ResNet-18 at negligible overhead. The directional gate protects the past task at no measured plasticity cost but at far higher compute, fails late and abruptly at its strongest diagnostic setting, and is re-inflated by momentum. Which gate is preferable is set by the momentum regime.
\end{abstract}
